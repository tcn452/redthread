I work at the intersection of textile art, creative writing, and craftivism — practices that have always done far more than decorate. 

In Chile during the Pinochet regime, women whose husbands, children, and brothers were disappeared began stitching *arpilleras* out of scraps of burlap and remnants of their loved ones' clothing. Under strict state censorship, these appliquéd tapestries recorded what official news outlets made unsayable: breadlines, military roundups, prison interrogations, and enduring solidarity. Smuggled out of the country, the arpilleras became evidence and an urgent cry to the international community.

In South Africa, testimony has long found its way into fabric, verse, and beadwork when the official record left too much out. That lineage is where my own practice sits.

### Craftivism: Making as Activism

Craftivism treats making as a form of active engagement:
- The object itself becomes the argument.
- The fabric preserves the evidence.
- The slow, deliberate rhythm of stitching creates an invitation to look closer and listen deeper.

My textile art draws directly on this tradition. In pieces like *Women of Gaza* and community workshop tapestries, cloth and needle give form to grief, endurance, and human dignity. 

> *"Craft and word have never been separate practices for me — they are two ways of doing the same thing."*

Alongside textile work, writing has run through my working life for decades:
- Editing a landmark collection of women’s prison writings.
- Running creative writing workshops across South Africa for the Congress of South African Writers (COSAW).
- Leading the editorial collective of a national writers’ magazine for six years.
- Publishing novels, biographies, and poetry.

When we bring communities together with needle, thread, and pen, we dismantle hierarchies. In our craftivism workshops, participants discover that they do not need formal credentials to bear witness. Their hands already know the story.
